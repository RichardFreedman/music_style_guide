### Fair Use and Intellectual Property

In the classroom we have wide latitude with respect to what is called the **Fair Use** of copyrighted materials like musical works and their derivatives (printed scores, sound recordings, etc). The same is true of images, films, or other multimedia materials that we might consider the context of the classroom. But particularly as we think about publishing works of criticism, history, and analysis, we must understand Fair Use in order to respect the intellectual property of others.

Under U.S. Copyright Law (which is *not* the same as European or other international standards), basic copyright for any work created after January 1, 1978 is normally "Author's Lifetime Plus 70 Years."  But of course in the case of a musical work there are many *layers* of rights. For a recent recording of a piano sonata by Beethoven, for instance:

- The **sonata itself is in the public domain** (and so in principle anyone could record, publish or transform it in any way they like without restriction)
- The **edition used in the recording might be subject to copyright** (if the less than 70 years old, for instance)
- The **recording itself would be subject to copyright** (unless made before 1972, before which time there was no protection for these derivative works; the actual law is very complex here, as you can read in the [US Copyright Act](https://www.copyright.gov/docs/sound/)).

And if you are working with a **popular song**, things are even more complex, since the **lyrics**, too, could be subject to copyright in the same way!  


### Fair Use and Scholarship

Fair Use is a thus a complex topic, and there are few hard-and-fast rules about exactly how much of a copyrighted work can be reproduced without obtaining explicit permission. Indeed, the concept of Fair Use hinges on the idea that permission is *not needed* provided scholars do the following:

>1. **Take from the copyrighted work only the minimum** needed to make their point. As a rule this means no more than ten percent of a sound or video recording, only a few measures of printed music, or short excerpts from prose. Song lyrics are a special case, as commercial interests are especially protective of these.
>2. **Transform the chosen excerpts** through the addition of analytical commentary, interpretive diagrams, or other adaptive work that adds intellectual or artistic value.
>3. **Explicitly identify the source** material in a formal citation. 


The **American Musicological Society** has published a more detailed [Fair Use guide](https://www.amsmusicology.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ams_fair_use_statement.pdf). Familiarize yourself with this and be sure to consult your professor or librarian with any questions concerning the use of copyrighted material in your writing.

### Finding Public Domain Scores and Images

Materials published more than one hundred years ago are normally in the public domain and can be used without restriction. Fair Use applies to anything more recent.  

For **scores** one reasoanble source of public domain sources [IMSLP](https://imslp.org/), a free archive of scores.  Most (but not all!) of these are in the *public domain*, which means that they are free for you to excerpt and publish. With the exception of those flagged as _Non-PD US_, you may use these editions in any way you like.  If you have questions, Haverford’s music librarian Guillermo Gómez (ggomez1@haverford.edu) will help you navigate results.

For **images** try various **Open Access** initiatives, such as that promoted by the [Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York City](https://www.metmuseum.org/hubs/open-access):

> In February 2017, The Met introduced its Open Access Initiative, which makes images of public-domain artworks and basic data on all accessioned works in its collection available for unrestricted use under Creative Commons Zero (CC0). Anyone can download, share, and remix images and data about artworks in The Met collection. It is also an important statement about The Met's commitment to increasing access to the collection in a digital age.

### The Parts of an Example, Figure or Media Clip

An example (or figure, audio, video, etc) has four components:

>1. **A parenthetical reference in the essay.** This links the reader to the example itself.
>2. **The example itself**, located in your paper in the case of a score, or via some linked repository in the case of audio or video (in Gdrive, Box)
>3. **A caption**, which goes immediately after the example, and explains its precise source or purpose
>4. A **bibliographical citation**, located in a list of examples or figures at the **end of the essay**. 

### Numbering the Examples

Examples are numbered consecutively regardless of medium (score, audio, or video).
